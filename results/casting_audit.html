What the inspection model actually learned

Three findings from auditing a casting defect classifier: the held-out set was mostly not held out, augmentation prevents a specific and visible failure, and the model sorted defects into types nobody labelled.

7,284 images~3,600 distinct castings 200 labelled blindtwo 100-epoch runs 16 Sept 2026
Finding 1

89% of the test set had a rotated twin in training. Hash checks cannot see rotation. The real test set is about 79 castings, not 715.

Finding 2

Augmentation stops the model memorising. Validation loss degrades 9.1× without it and 1.3× with it. The accuracy gap is inside the noise; the loss gap is not.

Finding 3

The model separates rim damage from face spots without being trained to. A human labelling blind reproduces the split.

Finding 1

The held-out set was mostly not held out

An earlier check compared SHA-256 hashes and found 64 validation images byte-identical to training images. That check is blind to rotation: turn an image one degree and every byte changes, so a hash reports two different files for what is plainly the same part.

Re-checking with a rotation-proof fingerprint — polar coordinates, then the magnitude of an FFT along the angle axis, which a rotation cannot change — finds far more. Each candidate is then verified by recovering the exact angle and measuring what is left after alignment.

Finding 2

Augmentation stops the model memorising

Two 100-epoch runs, identical except that one augments the training images with flips, rotation and colour jitter. Pretrained AlexNet, batch 16, Adam at 1e-4, the same leak-free split. Validation is never augmented.

no augmentation augmented solid = validation · dashed = training

The two dashed lines tell the story. Without augmentation, training loss falls to 5×10-6 — the model has memorised all 6,569 training images — and from epoch 34 its validation loss climbs for the remaining 66 epochs. With augmentation the model never sees the same image twice, training loss settles at 0.0063, and validation loss stays flat at its floor.

Read the accuracy row as noise

Two errors against zero on 715 samples is well inside sampling variation — the 95% intervals overlap. The loss comparison is not noise: a 9.1× degradation against 1.3×, with the mechanism visible in the curve, is a large effect in the direction theory predicts.

The caveat Finding 1 creates

The validation set is largely made of rotated copies of training images, and the augmentation being tested is rotation. That is the exact inductive bias which would help on this particular test set for reasons unrelated to generalising to new castings. The loss-curve result stands on its own — it is measured on training dynamics, not on the contaminated split — but the accuracy comparison needs re-running on a group-aware split before it means anything.

The two castings the unaugmented model missed

Both are defective; both were called good. The same two failed every unaugmented run. Neither has a rotated twin anywhere in the dataset — they are among the genuinely novel castings, which is why they were hard.

At 15 epochs the model called them wrong at 0.94 and 0.79 confidence, low enough that a review threshold would have caught both. At 100 epochs it called them wrong at p = 1.000. Overfitting did not just fail to fix the errors — it destroyed the uncertainty that made them catchable. For an inspection system that is the more expensive failure.

Finding 3

The model sorted defects into types nobody labelled

Stripping the final classifier off the trained model exposes a 4,096-number description of each casting. Clustering those splits the 4,211 defective castings in two. To find out what the split means, 200 were labelled by hand with no indication of which group they came from.

The four defect types

Frayed edge

rim damage 42.5%

The rim is ragged or torn along its length — a continuous run of disturbed edge, not a single bite out of it.

Chip

rim damage 14.5%

Material missing from the edge: a discrete bite or notch with its own boundary, in one place.

Slash

rim damage 35.0%

A linear mark — scratch, gouge or drag line, longer than it is wide.

Spot on the face

face damage 48.5%

A blemish on the flat face away from the rim — porosity, an inclusion, a blotch. The rim itself is usually clean.

A model merely ranking parts from lightly to badly damaged would push every row the same direction. Collapsed to families: any rim damage — A 77%, B 41% against face spot — A 40%, B 57%.

Ruling out “just more damaged”

Take only the 111 castings with exactly one defect. Every part in that set carries the same amount of damage, so severity is held constant by construction and anything left is about type.

Group A is 45% face spots, group B is 73% — p = 0.003. The split survives with severity fixed.

Fifteen castings with nothing wrong

Every image in this study is labelled defective in the published dataset. On 15 of 200 — about 10% of all defective castings once scaled — no defect was visible. Either those labels carry noise or the defect is too subtle to see at this resolution. Both belong next to any accuracy figure: a classifier cannot be separating defective from good more cleanly than the labels themselves distinguish them.

The first measurement got this backwards

An earlier round asked one question covering “spots, chips or nicks” together. It was true of 89% of castings — far too common to separate anything — and it pointed the opposite way to its own spot component, because chips and slashes lean toward group A and outvoted spots, which lean toward B.

As asked: A 96% / B 81%. The spot part alone, once separated: A 40% / B 57%. A combined variable inverted a real effect, and the conclusion drawn from it — that the model tracked severity — was wrong.

That round was not wasted. Its frayed-edge question used the same narrow wording both times and replicates: +22.5 points then, +31.0 now, pooling to +28.3, p ≈ 0.000001 over 298 castings. Seven castings were drawn in both rounds and labelled twice, blind, weeks apart — called the same way 7 of 7.

What this supports, and what it does not

Supported

The dataset contains large-scale rotational duplication that hash comparison cannot detect. Augmentation prevents a measurable and mechanically explained overfitting failure. The model learned a rim-versus-face defect distinction unsupervised, and a blind human sort reproduces it.

Not supported

Any accuracy figure quoted against the published split, including 715 of 715. That augmentation improves accuracy — the gap is inside the noise and the test set favours it. A stage-two defect classifier trained on the model's own groupings: those groups are roughly a 65%-accurate stand-in for real defect type, 12 points better than guessing, so such a model would score near 99% against the groups and near 65% against reality.

Next

Rebuild the split so no duplicate family spans train and validation, then retrain both runs and re-measure. That number is the project's real headline and it will be lower. For defect typing, hand labels are required rather than cluster labels: 155 unambiguous ones already exist, enough for a pilot; roughly 1,500 labelled castings would cover frayed edges, spots and slashes properly, with chips rare enough at 14.5% to need far more or to be folded into a broader rim-damage flag.

Two 100-epoch AlexNet runs, pretrained, batch 16, Adam 1e-4, differing only in augmentation. Duplicate detection by rotation-invariant polar-FFT fingerprint with phase-correlation alignment, thresholded below a per-class measured null. 200 castings labelled blind across two rounds with the primary hypothesis fixed before labelling; Fisher exact tests throughout, secondary flags held to a corrected threshold. Example castings are real parts from the labelled sample, each showing a single defect type except where noted.
